\section{Port-Hamiltonian dynamics}

Consider a physical system with state $x\in\mathbb R^n$ interacting
with an environment of state $z\in\mathbb R^m$. The two subsystems store
energy $H_x(x)$ and $H_z(z)$, respectively. We first assume that the
coupling stores no additional energy, so that
\begin{equation}
H(x,z) = H_x(x) + H_z(z).
\end{equation}
The gradients of $H$ are the generalized efforts. If the coupling itself
stores energy, its degrees of freedom must be included in $x$ or $z$.

Each subsystem has internal dynamics and ports through which it
interacts with other processes:
\begin{align}
\dot x
&=
[\mathbf J(x)-\mathbf R(x)]\nabla_x H
+\mathbf B_x(x)u_x
+\mathbf G(x)u,
\label{eq:x}\\
\dot z
&=
[\mathbf J(z)-\mathbf R(z)]\nabla_z H
+\mathbf B_z(z)u_z.
\label{eq:z}
\end{align}
For each subsystem,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J^\top = -\mathbf J,
\qquad
\mathbf R^\top = \mathbf R \succeq 0.
\end{equation}
The skew part $\mathbf J$ describes reversible motion and $\mathbf R$
describes intrinsic dissipation. The matrices $\mathbf B_x$ and
$\mathbf B_z$ specify the environmental ports, while $\mathbf G$
specifies a separate port used to regulate $x$
\cite{vanderschaft2014}. Their conjugate outputs are
\begin{align}
y_x &= \mathbf B_x^\top(x)\nabla_x H,\\
y_z &= \mathbf B_z^\top(z)\nabla_z H,\\
y   &= \mathbf G^\top(x)\nabla_x H.
\label{eq:y}
\end{align}
Each input-output pair defines a power exchange, with $y_x^\top u_x$,
$y_z^\top u_z$, and $y^\top u$ measured in units of power.

We connect the environmental ports by
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
u_x\\ u_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\left[
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \mathbf C\\
-\mathbf C^\top & 0
\end{bmatrix}
-\mathbf D
\right]
\begin{bmatrix}
y_x\\ y_z
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf D = \mathbf D^\top \succeq 0.
\label{eq:interconnection}
\end{equation}
The matrices $\mathbf C$ and $\mathbf D$ may depend on $(x,z)$.
The skew connection $\mathbf C$ transfers energy without losses, whereas
$\mathbf D$ accounts for dissipation at the connection, including
dissipation involving both subsystems. Choosing $\mathbf B_x=\mathbf
B_z=\mathbf I$ and $\mathbf D=0$ recovers the direct lossless coupling.

The resulting energy balance is
\begin{align}
\dot H
={}&
-\nabla_x H^\top\mathbf R(x)\nabla_x H
-\nabla_z H^\top\mathbf R(z)\nabla_z H
\nonumber\\
&-
\begin{bmatrix}y_x\\y_z\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}
\mathbf D
\begin{bmatrix}y_x\\y_z\end{bmatrix}
+y^\top u.
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
Intrinsic and interconnection dissipation both lower the total energy.
The skew connection cancels from the balance. The remaining power
$y^\top u$ enters through the regulatory port; it must be accounted
for if that port is closed by a physical feedback mechanism.

\section{Constrained feedback}

Let $g(x)$ collect the physical quantities to be regulated after
subtracting their desired values. Examples include a temperature error,
a concentration imbalance, a pressure difference, or a mechanical
displacement from an operating configuration. The homeostatic operating
surface is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal M
=
\{x:g(x)=0\},
\qquad
g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r.
\end{equation}
If $x$ starts on $\mathcal M$, invariance requires
\begin{equation}
\dot g
=
\partial_x g\,\dot x
=
0.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:x}, define the drift of $x$ with its
environmental connection in place but without regulatory input,
\begin{equation}
f(x,z)
=
[\mathbf J(x)-\mathbf R(x)]\nabla_x H
+\mathbf B_x(x)u_x.
\label{eq:f}
\end{equation}
Here $u_x$ is determined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:interconnection}. The
invariance condition is then
\begin{equation}
\partial_x g\,\mathbf G\,u
=
-\partial_x g\,f(x,z).
\label{eq:constraint}
\end{equation}

Define the map from the regulatory port to the regulated observables,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A(x)
=
\partial_x g(x)\mathbf G(x).
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
If $\mathbf A$ has full row rank, the available actuators can correct
every instantaneous direction that moves the regulated observables away
from their target. The general control is
\begin{equation}
u
=
-\mathbf A^\dagger\partial_x g\,f
+
(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^\dagger\mathbf A)v.
\label{eq:u}
\end{equation}
The first term is the smallest port input that cancels the transverse
drift. The second changes motion only in directions invisible to the
regulated observables.

Exact invariance assumes that the system already lies on the desired
operating surface. A homeostatic restoring law is instead
\begin{equation}
\dot g
=
-\mathbf K g,
\qquad
\mathbf K\succ0,
\end{equation}
which gives
\begin{equation}
u
=
-\mathbf A^\dagger
\left[
\partial_x g\,f+\mathbf K g
\right]
+
(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^\dagger\mathbf A)v.
\label{eq:restoring}
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of $\mathbf K$ set the physical relaxation rates of the
regulated quantities.

We now restrict the control to a self-feedback through the same port,
\begin{equation}
u
=
\mathbf Q(x,z)y,
\qquad
y
=
\mathbf G^\top(x)\nabla_x H.
\label{eq:self}
\end{equation}
The constraint becomes
\begin{equation}
\partial_x g
\left[
f(x,z)+\mathbf G(x)\mathbf Q(x,z)y
\right]
=
0.
\label{eq:Qconstraint}
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathbf Q$ converts the generalized effort measured at the port
into the flow or force required to maintain the regulated observables.
For $y\neq0$, one map producing a required input $u$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf Q
=
\frac{u y^\top}{y^\top y},
\label{eq:Q}
\end{equation}
although this map is not unique. If $y=0$, an algebraic self-feedback of
this form cannot generate a nonzero correction.

The energetic effect of the feedback follows directly from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy},
\begin{equation}
y^\top u
=
y^\top\mathbf Qy.
\label{eq:Qpower}
\end{equation}
If $\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$, the feedback is lossless and only
redistributes power between port directions. A negative symmetric part
removes energy. A positive symmetric part injects energy and therefore
requires an explicit supply. The matrix $\mathbf Q$ is a routing law,
not an energy source. If the feedback draws energy from the environment,
that energy must be stored in or supplied through physical degrees of
freedom such as $z$.

This construction is close to the original cybernetic notion of
homeostasis. Ashby's essential variables are quantities that must remain
inside a viable range; the equality $g(x)=0$ is the sharp local version
of such a requirement, while $\dot g=-\mathbf K g$ describes active
return toward the viable set \cite{ashby1956}. The port-Hamiltonian form
adds information that is absent from an abstract regulator diagram: it
specifies which variables can actually be actuated, which exchanges are
lossless, where dissipation occurs, and how much power the regulator
must supply.

The connection to the good regulator theorem is more specific.
Conant and Ashby showed that, under their optimality assumptions, a
successful and simple regulator must embody a model of the system being
regulated \cite{conant1970}. The algebraic map $\mathbf Q$ alone is not
such a model: it has no internal state and only specifies an instantaneous
conversion from output to input. In the present physical formulation,
the natural carrier of an internal model is the dynamical state $z$.
Its Hamiltonian, dissipation, and coupling can reproduce the system or
environmental modes that matter for regulation. This is also the
physical analogue of the later internal model principle, in which robust
regulation requires the feedback dynamics to contain the relevant
dynamics of the disturbances or reference signals \cite{francis1976}.

The resulting question is therefore not only whether a regulator exists,
but which regulator models are physically realizable. A candidate
internal model must fit inside a dynamical subsystem with finite stored
energy, admissible dissipation, and ports capable of supplying the power
required by Eq.~\eqref{eq:Qpower}. This turns the cybernetic requirement
for a model into an energetic and geometric constraint on the regulator.
