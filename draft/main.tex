\section{A system with two kinds of ports}

Consider a physical system with state $x\in\mathbb R^n$ interacting
with an environment of state $z\in\mathbb R^m$. Their stored energies
are $H_x(x)$ and $H_z(z)$, with $H=H_x+H_z$. Any energy stored in a
connection is included in these states. We write their port-Hamiltonian
dynamics as
\begin{align}
\dot x
&= (\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)\nabla_x H
+\mathbf B_x u_x+\mathbf G u,
\label{eq:x}\\
\dot z
&= (\mathbf J_z-\mathbf R_z)\nabla_z H
+\mathbf B_z u_z,
\label{eq:z}
\end{align}
where the matrices may depend on their respective states, and
\begin{equation}
\mathbf J_x^\top=-\mathbf J_x,\qquad
\mathbf J_z^\top=-\mathbf J_z,\qquad
\mathbf R_x=\mathbf R_x^\top\succeq0,\qquad
\mathbf R_z=\mathbf R_z^\top\succeq0.
\end{equation}
The skew matrices describe reversible internal motion and the positive
semidefinite matrices describe internal dissipation
\cite{vanderschaft2014}.

The matrix $\mathbf G(x)$ defines a self-feedback port on $x$, with
input $u$ and conjugate output $y$. The matrices $\mathbf B_x(x)$
and $\mathbf B_z(z)$ define the two sides of a separate connection
between the system and its environment. Their conjugate outputs are
\begin{equation}
y=\mathbf G^\top\nabla_x H,\qquad
y_x=\mathbf B_x^\top\nabla_x H,\qquad
y_z=\mathbf B_z^\top\nabla_z H.
\label{eq:outputs}
\end{equation}
The self-feedback closes a relation between $u$ and $y$ within $x$.
The environmental inputs $u_x$ and $u_z$ are instead determined by
the connection between $(u_x,y_x)$ and $(u_z,y_z)$. We leave this
connection unspecified: it may describe lossless exchange,
dissipative transport, or a driven process.

The energy balance follows directly from the two subsystems,
\begin{align}
\dot H ={}&
-(\nabla_x H)^\top\mathbf R_x\nabla_x H
-(\nabla_z H)^\top\mathbf R_z\nabla_z H
\nonumber\\
&+y^\top u+y_x^\top u_x+y_z^\top u_z.
\label{eq:energy}
\end{align}
The last three terms are port powers, not prescribed sources or
sinks. Their values depend on how the ports are connected.

\section{The constraint and available actuation}

Let $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r$ describe regulated observables
relative to their target values. The operating surface is
\begin{equation}
M=\{x:g(x)=0\}.
\end{equation}
We assume that $\partial_x g(x)$ has full row rank near $M$, so the surface
is locally smooth. Invariance of $M$ requires $\dot g=0$ for
every initial condition on it. Define the drift of $x$ without self-feedback, for a given
environmental input $u_x$, and the map from the self-feedback port
to the observables,
\begin{equation}
f(x,u_x)=(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)\nabla_x H+\mathbf B_x u_x,\qquad
\mathbf A(x)=\partial_x g(x)\mathbf G(x).
\label{eq:drift}
\end{equation}
Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:x} gives the exact tangency condition
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A(x)u=-\partial_x g(x)f(x,u_x),\qquad x\in M.
\label{eq:tangency}
\end{equation}
A regulating input exists at a given $(x,z)$ precisely when
$-\partial_x g\,f$ belongs to the image of $\mathbf A$. Full row rank of $\mathbf A$ guarantees
solvability for any transverse drift. Under this assumption the
inputs have the form
\begin{equation}
u=-\mathbf A^\dagger \partial_x g\,f+(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^\dagger \mathbf A)w,
\label{eq:controls}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf A^\dagger$ is the Moore--Penrose inverse. The first term is
the minimum Euclidean-norm correction; the second changes the motion
without changing $\dot g$. This is instantaneous invariance, not
attraction to $M$ or a stability result for the coupled system.

\section{Feedback and power}

To describe an algebraic feedback through the measured port, write
\begin{equation}
u=\mathbf Q(x)y,\qquad y=\mathbf G^\top \nabla_x H.
\label{eq:feedback}
\end{equation}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:tangency} and \eqref{eq:feedback},
the feedback must satisfy
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A\mathbf Q y=-\partial_x g\,f.
\label{eq:qconstraint}
\end{equation}
For $y\ne0$, any feasible input $u$ can be represented pointwise by
$\mathbf Q=u y^\top/(y^\top y)$. This does not construct a single
$x$-dependent feedback law valid for every environmental input,
nor establish physical realizability. Its power is
\begin{equation}
y^\top u=y^\top \mathbf Q_s y,\qquad
\mathbf Q_s=\tfrac12(\mathbf Q+\mathbf Q^\top).
\label{eq:feedbackpower}
\end{equation}
The skew part of $\mathbf Q$ transfers power between port components but
cannot inject energy. An input requiring $y^\top u>0$ cannot be
realized by skew feedback alone. If $y=0$, the law $u=\mathbf Q y$ cannot
produce any nonzero correction, even when Eq.~\eqref{eq:tangency}
admits one.

The two requirements can conflict even when the actuation matrix
has full row rank. Let $u_c=-\mathbf A^\dagger \partial_x g\,f$. A power-neutral
correction requires both $\mathbf A u=-\partial_x g\,f$ and $y^\top u=0$. When
$y^\top(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^\dagger \mathbf A)\ne0$, the free term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:controls} can make the correction power-neutral.
Otherwise its power is fixed at $y^\top u_c$, so a lossless
correction exists only if this quantity vanishes. For $y\ne0$
and $y^\top u=0$, the skew map
\begin{equation}
\mathbf Q=\frac{u y^\top-y u^\top}{y^\top y}
\label{eq:skew}
\end{equation}
indeed satisfies $\mathbf Q y=u$. These are algebraic feasibility
statements; they do not imply that $\mathbf Q$ can be implemented by
an autonomous regulator.

Closing the ports requires a physical connection law. The
self-feedback contributes $y^\top u$ and the environmental connection
contributes $y_x^\top u_x+y_z^\top u_z$. If these connections have
no unmodeled energy supply or storage and are passive as a whole,
their combined power must satisfy
\begin{equation}
y^\top u+y_x^\top u_x+y_z^\top u_z\leq0.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
This is a conditional energy requirement, not a definition of the
connections. Positive power entering through the self-feedback
port must be accounted for by another physical process; choosing
$\mathbf Q$ alone does not supply it.

If $H$ is bounded below by $H_{\min}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:closure}
also gives the finite-energy restriction
\begin{equation}
\int_0^T \left[
(\nabla_x H)^\top \mathbf R_x\nabla_x H
+(\nabla_z H)^\top \mathbf R_z\nabla_z H
\right]\,dt
\leq H(0)-H_{\min}.
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Thus an isolated, finite-energy regulator cannot support strictly
positive dissipation indefinitely. Sustained homeostasis against
persistent losses requires an energy supply represented in the
model, rather than hidden in the feedback matrix.

\section{Self-maintaining regulation}

Preserving $g(x)=0$ at one instant does not guarantee that
self-feedback will remain possible later. We impose a minimal
self-maintenance requirement: the actual motion must preserve both
the constraint and the existence of an admissible self-feedback
that can continue to enforce it. This distinguishes the motion
from the physical conditions that permit that motion
\cite{montevil2015}.

To obtain a definite restriction, first consider a lossless
self-feedback, $\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$. The $x$ dynamics can
then be written as
\begin{equation}
\dot x=(\mathbf P-\mathbf R_x)\nabla_x H+\mathbf B_x u_x,
\qquad
\mathbf P=\mathbf J_x+\mathbf G\mathbf Q\mathbf G^\top.
\label{eq:structure}
\end{equation}
The matrix $\mathbf P$ is skew-symmetric and defines the
reversible part of the closed $x$ dynamics. It defines a
Poisson bracket only if it also satisfies the Jacobi identity;
skew symmetry alone does not suffice. With the bracket notation
$\{a,H\}_{\mathbf P}=\partial_x a\,\mathbf P\nabla_x H$,
preservation of the operating surface requires
\begin{equation}
\{g,H\}_{\mathbf P}
=
\partial_x g\,(\mathbf R_x\nabla_x H-\mathbf B_xu_x).
\label{eq:poissonconstraint}
\end{equation}
The reversible motion must cancel the transverse drift
caused by dissipation and environmental exchange.

Not every such cancellation can be produced by lossless
self-feedback. At a state with $y\ne0$, skew symmetry of
$\mathbf Q$ implies $y^\top u=0$. Conversely, every input
orthogonal to $y$ can be written as $u=\mathbf Qy$ for some
skew-symmetric matrix. Define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf L(x)
=\mathbf A(x)\left(\mathbf I-\frac{yy^\top}{y^\top y}\right),
\qquad
c(x,z)=(\mathbf I-\mathbf L\mathbf L^\dagger)
\partial_x g\,f(x,u_x).
\label{eq:obstruction}
\end{equation}
Here we assume that the environmental connection supplies
smooth inputs $u_x(x,z)$, without choosing a particular
connection law. The condition $c(x,z)=0$ is necessary and
sufficient for the existence of a \emph{pointwise} lossless
self-feedback input satisfying Eq.~\eqref{eq:tangency}.
It does not guarantee a single smooth law $\mathbf Q(x)$
that works across different environmental states. In
particular, if $y=0$, algebraic self-feedback cannot produce
a nonzero input, and feasibility instead requires
$\partial_x g\,f=0$.

The states where the constraint and the lossless correction
are simultaneously feasible form
\begin{equation}
K=\{(x,z):g(x)=0,\ c(x,z)=0\}.
\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}
For $y\ne0$ and constant rank of $\mathbf L$, the obstruction
$c$ is smooth. A self-maintaining closed dynamics must leave
$K$ invariant, not merely start inside it. Writing its
vector field as $(F_x,F_z)$, a necessary condition is
\begin{equation}
\partial_x c\,F_x+\partial_z c\,F_z=0,
\qquad (x,z)\in K.
\label{eq:selfmaintenance}
\end{equation}
Together with $\dot g=0$, this is the tangency condition
for the continued feasibility of regulation. When $K$ is a
regular submanifold and the closed vector field is smooth,
tangency everywhere on $K$ also gives local invariance
\cite{aubin2009}. The second condition is not automatic
from the first: dynamics can preserve the regulated
observable while leaving the set where future corrections
are available.

Equations~\eqref{eq:obstruction} and
\eqref{eq:selfmaintenance} supply an algebraic obstruction
and a dynamical compatibility condition. They express a
minimal form of self-maintenance, not yet the preservation
of every admissible motion or a general theorem of
organizational closure. The obstruction may vanish
identically when the available self-port directions are
sufficient; in that case this criterion imposes no further
restriction beyond constraint invariance.

\section{Interpretation}

The operating surface $M$ is a local representation of the
essential variables in Ashby's account of regulation
\cite{ashby1956}. The good regulator theorem links effective
regulation to an internal model under its own assumptions
\cite{conant1970}. Here $x$ has a self-feedback mechanism and $z$ represents its
environment. Neither the feedback matrix $\mathbf Q$ nor the state
$z$ by itself establishes an internal model in the sense of the
theorem or of the internal model principle \cite{francis1976}.

The immediate problem is to determine whether a closed $x$--$z$
dynamics can satisfy both the tangency condition
\eqref{eq:tangency} and the power condition \eqref{eq:closure},
and whether the resulting trajectories remain near $M$ under
perturbations. The present equations identify the geometric and
energetic requirements, but do not yet provide a dynamical
regulation theorem or a new physical mechanism.
