\section{Port-Hamiltonian dynamics}

We start with a state $x \in \mathbb R^n$ and an energy function
$H_x : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$. A port-Hamiltonian system with one
external port is
\begin{equation}
    \dot x
    =
    \left[ J_x(x) - R_x(x) \right] \nabla H_x(x)
    + G(x) u,
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    J_x^\top = -J_x,
    \qquad
    R_x^\top = R_x \succeq 0.
\end{equation}
The matrix $J_x$ redistributes energy without changing the total stored
energy, while $R_x$ dissipates energy.

The input $u \in \mathbb R^p$ acts through the port matrix
$G(x) \in \mathbb R^{n \times p}$. The conjugate output is
\begin{equation}
    y
    =
    G^\top(x) \nabla H_x(x).
\end{equation}
This definition follows directly from the energy balance. Along the
dynamics,
\begin{align}
    \dot H_x
    &=
    \nabla H_x^\top \dot x
    \nonumber\\
    &=
    - \nabla H_x^\top R_x \nabla H_x
    + y^\top u.
\end{align}
The scalar $y^\top u$ is therefore the power entering the $x$ system
through the port. Positive values inject energy and negative values remove
energy.

\section{Coupling to a second system}

We now introduce a second state $z \in \mathbb R^m$ with energy
$H_z(z)$. We keep the two systems explicit and couple them through a
matrix $C(x,z) \in \mathbb R^{n \times m}$,
\begin{align}
    \dot x
    &=
    \left[ J_x - R_x \right] \nabla H_x
    + C \nabla H_z
    + G u,
    \label{eq:x}\\
    \dot z
    &=
    \left[ J_z - R_z \right] \nabla H_z
    - C^\top \nabla H_x,
    \label{eq:z}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
    J_z^\top = -J_z,
    \qquad
    R_z^\top = R_z \succeq 0.
\end{equation}
Arguments such as $J_x(x)$ and $C(x,z)$ are omitted when they are
unambiguous.

The opposite signs in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:x}--\eqref{eq:z} make the coupling
power preserving. For the total stored energy
\begin{equation}
    H(x,z) = H_x(x) + H_z(z),
\end{equation}
the coupling terms cancel,
\begin{equation}
    \nabla H_x^\top C \nabla H_z
    -
    \nabla H_z^\top C^\top \nabla H_x
    = 0.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
    \dot H
    =
    - \nabla H_x^\top R_x \nabla H_x
    - \nabla H_z^\top R_z \nabla H_z
    + y^\top u.
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
The $x$--$z$ coupling only transfers energy between the two systems.
Dissipation occurs through $R_x$ and $R_z$, while the port $(u,y)$ can
exchange energy with whatever is connected to it.

For the constraint calculation it is useful to collect the uncontrolled
$x$ dynamics into
\begin{equation}
    f_x(x,z)
    =
    \left[ J_x(x) - R_x(x) \right] \nabla H_x(x)
    + C(x,z) \nabla H_z(z).
    \label{eq:fx}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:x} then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \dot x = f_x(x,z) + G(x)u.
    \label{eq:xcompact}
\end{equation}
The $z$ dynamics remain explicitly given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:z}.

\section{Constraint on the $x$ dynamics}

Let
\begin{equation}
    g : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^r
\end{equation}
define a set of observables of the $x$ configuration. We want the
dynamics to remain on the manifold
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal M
    =
    \left\{
        x \in \mathbb R^n :
        g(x) = 0
    \right\}.
\end{equation}
Assume that $Dg(x)$ has full row rank near $\mathcal M$, so that
$\mathcal M$ is locally a smooth manifold of codimension $r$.

If a trajectory starts on $\mathcal M$, invariance requires
\begin{equation}
    \dot g
    =
    Dg(x) \dot x
    =
    0.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:xcompact},
\begin{equation}
    Dg(x) f_x(x,z)
    +
    Dg(x)G(x)u
    =
    0.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
    A(x)
    =
    Dg(x)G(x)
    \in
    \mathbb R^{r \times p}.
    \label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
The control input must therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
    A(x)u
    =
    -Dg(x)f_x(x,z).
    \label{eq:constraintcontrol}
\end{equation}

If $A$ has full row rank, every instantaneous constraint violation can be
corrected through the port. The minimum-norm correction is
\begin{equation}
    u_c
    =
    -A^\dagger Dg\, f_x,
    \label{eq:uc}
\end{equation}
where $A^\dagger$ is the Moore--Penrose inverse. More generally,
\begin{equation}
    u
    =
    -A^\dagger Dg\, f_x
    +
    \left( I - A^\dagger A \right)v.
    \label{eq:ugeneral}
\end{equation}
The second term lies in $\ker A$. It can modify the microscopic dynamics
without changing $\dot g$.

If $A$ does not have full row rank, Eq.~\eqref{eq:constraintcontrol} can
still be solved when $-Dg\,f_x$ belongs to the image of $A$. Full row
rank is the stronger condition that guarantees solvability for every
constraint-violating direction.

\section{Attraction toward the constraint manifold}

Exact invariance assumes that the initial state already satisfies
$g(x)=0$. A restoring dynamics can instead be imposed directly in the
observable coordinates,
\begin{equation}
    \dot g
    =
    -K_g g,
    \qquad
    K_g \succ 0.
    \label{eq:restoring}
\end{equation}
Substituting the $x$ dynamics gives
\begin{equation}
    A u
    =
    -Dg\,f_x
    -K_g g.
\end{equation}
The corresponding family of controls is
\begin{equation}
    u
    =
    -A^\dagger
    \left(
        Dg\,f_x + K_g g
    \right)
    +
    \left( I - A^\dagger A \right)v.
    \label{eq:urestore}
\end{equation}
On the manifold, $g=0$, this reduces to the invariance condition above.
Away from the manifold, the controller also supplies the correction
needed to drive $g$ toward zero.

\section{Self-feedback through the port}

The control $u$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:xcompact} has so far been arbitrary.
For a self-feedback law we require the input to be generated from the
conjugate output
\begin{equation}
    y
    =
    G^\top \nabla H_x.
\end{equation}
The simplest linear relation at each state is
\begin{equation}
    u
    =
    Q(x,z)y,
    \label{eq:feedback}
\end{equation}
where $Q(x,z) \in \mathbb R^{p \times p}$. The $x$ dynamics become
\begin{equation}
    \dot x
    =
    f_x(x,z)
    +
    G Q G^\top \nabla H_x.
\end{equation}

To maintain the constraint, $Q$ must generate a control vector satisfying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:constraintcontrol}. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    A Q y
    =
    -Dg\,f_x.
    \label{eq:Qconstraint}
\end{equation}
If $u_c$ is any control satisfying the constraint, the feedback problem
reduces to
\begin{equation}
    Qy = u_c.
    \label{eq:Qmap}
\end{equation}
For $y \neq 0$, one simple solution is
\begin{equation}
    Q
    =
    \frac{u_c y^\top}{y^\top y}.
    \label{eq:Qsolution}
\end{equation}
This is not the unique solution. It only fixes the action of $Q$ along
the current output direction $y$.

If $y=0$, an algebraic self-feedback of the form $u=Qy$ cannot produce a
nonzero correction. Constraint preservation at such a point therefore
requires
\begin{equation}
    Dg\,f_x = 0
\end{equation}
unless an additional input or dynamical controller is introduced.

\section{Energy required by the constraint}

The physical role of $Q$ follows from the power balance. Under the
self-feedback law,
\begin{equation}
    y^\top u
    =
    y^\top Qy.
\end{equation}
Decompose
\begin{equation}
    Q
    =
    Q_a + Q_s,
    \qquad
    Q_a
    =
    \frac{Q-Q^\top}{2},
    \qquad
    Q_s
    =
    \frac{Q+Q^\top}{2}.
\end{equation}
Since $y^\top Q_a y=0$,
\begin{equation}
    y^\top Qy
    =
    y^\top Q_s y.
    \label{eq:Qpower}
\end{equation}
The antisymmetric part of $Q$ is power preserving. A negative
semidefinite symmetric part removes energy, while a positive direction of
$Q_s$ injects energy.

The constraint condition and the energetic condition are therefore
different. Equation~\eqref{eq:Qconstraint} determines which control is
needed to maintain $g$. Equation~\eqref{eq:Qpower} determines the power
required to realize that control. A purely power-preserving self-loop,
$Q^\top=-Q$, can enforce the constraint only when the required correction
satisfies
\begin{equation}
    y^\top u_c = 0.
\end{equation}
A general constraint controller may instead require positive or negative
port power.

In a physical model, $Q$ should not be interpreted as an energy source
by itself. If the required feedback injects energy into $x$, that energy
must come from another subsystem or an external supply. The state $z$
provides a natural place to represent such an environment or controller.
The combined energy $H_x+H_z$ then keeps track of energy transferred
between the system and its environment. An algebraic $Q$ can be viewed
as an effective feedback law, while an explicit dynamical model for $z$
is needed when the source, storage, and depletion of that energy are part
of the problem.
