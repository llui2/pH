\title{Self-maintaining motion in port-Hamiltonian systems}
\maketitle

\section{System and environment}

Consider a physical system with state $x\in\mathbb R^n$ and an
environment with state $z\in\mathbb R^m$. Their stored energies are
$H_x(x)$ and $H_z(z)$, with $H=H_x+H_z$. Degrees of freedom that
store energy in a connection are included in these states.
We write
\begin{align}
\dot x
&=(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)\nabla_x H
+\mathbf B_xu_x+\mathbf G u,
\label{eq:x}\\
\dot z
&=(\mathbf J_z-\mathbf R_z)\nabla_z H
+\mathbf B_zu_z,
\label{eq:z}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf J_x$ and $\mathbf J_z$ are skew-symmetric and
$\mathbf R_x,\mathbf R_z$ are symmetric positive semidefinite.
The skew matrices describe reversible motion; the other terms
describe dissipation and exchange \cite{vanderschaft2014}.
Skew symmetry alone does not ensure a Poisson bracket:
its Jacobi identity must also hold.

The pair $(u,y)$ is a self-feedback port on $x$, while
$(u_x,y_x)$ and $(u_z,y_z)$ are the two sides of its
environmental connection. Their conjugate outputs are
\begin{equation}
y=\mathbf G^\top\nabla_x H,\qquad
y_x=\mathbf B_x^\top\nabla_x H,\qquad
y_z=\mathbf B_z^\top\nabla_z H.
\label{eq:outputs}
\end{equation}
No interconnection law is assumed between the environmental
ports. A possible self-feedback is $u=\mathbf Q(x)y$; we impose
no skew-symmetry or dissipativity condition on $\mathbf Q$.
More general feedback laws can be used without changing the
open-system description.

The skew part of $\mathbf Q$ changes the reversible motion
without contributing port power; its symmetric part determines
$y^\top u$.

The total energy balance is
\begin{align}
\dot H={}&
-(\nabla_x H)^\top\mathbf R_x\nabla_x H
-(\nabla_z H)^\top\mathbf R_z\nabla_z H \nonumber\\
&+y^\top u+y_x^\top u_x+y_z^\top u_z.
\label{eq:power}
\end{align}
Thus feedback may inject or dissipate power. Its physical
realization must account for that power through the actual
connection or another explicitly represented source.

\section{Viable motion}

Let $V\subset\mathbb R^{n+m}$ be a closed set of
system--environment states where the mechanisms needed for the
motion remain functional. The set $V$ specifies the physical
conditions of continued operation, such as admissible operating
ranges, available resources, and active exchange pathways.

Once the feedback and the environmental connection are fixed,
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:x}--\eqref{eq:z} define a vector field.
For the entire set $V$ to remain viable, that field must satisfy
\begin{equation}
(\dot x,\dot z)\in T_V(x,z),
\qquad (x,z)\in V,
\label{eq:viability}
\end{equation}
where $T_V$ is the contingent tangent cone of $V$.
For a locally Lipschitz vector field with forward solutions,
this condition is also sufficient for forward invariance
under the usual Nagumo hypotheses \cite{aubin2009}.
Requiring it on all of $V$ is stronger than demanding a single
trajectory remain viable.

For a regular equality constraint $g(x)=0$, the tangent
condition reads
\begin{equation}
\partial_x g\left[
(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)\nabla_x H
+\mathbf B_xu_x+\mathbf G u
\right]=0.
\label{eq:constraint}
\end{equation}
For a viable region specified by a smooth inequality,
the velocity must point inward or tangent at its boundary.

A stronger, structural requirement is that the same constraint
be preserved for every local energy gradient and every
independent environmental input $u_x$. With the collocated
self-feedback $u=\mathbf Q(x)y$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:constraint}
then reduces, on $g(x)=0$, to
\begin{align}
\partial_x g\left(
\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x+\mathbf G\mathbf Q\mathbf G^\top
\right)&=0,
\label{eq:structural}\\
\partial_x g\,\mathbf B_x&=0.
\label{eq:environment}
\end{align}
The first identity concerns internal dynamics and feedback;
the second requires an unrestricted environmental input to
act tangentially. This is structural controlled invariance,
a stronger requirement than viability for one Hamiltonian
and one environmental connection \cite{vanderschaft1985}.

The first identity has a direct algebraic compatibility
criterion. A matrix $\mathbf Q$, without restrictions on
its symmetric part, exists at a given state if and only if
\begin{align}
\operatorname{im}\left[
\partial_x g(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)
\right]
&\subseteq \operatorname{im}(\partial_x g\,\mathbf G),
\label{eq:actuation}\\
\ker\mathbf G^\top
&\subseteq
\ker\left[\partial_x g(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)\right].
\label{eq:observation}
\end{align}
Indeed, Eq.~\eqref{eq:structural} requires the matrix
$\partial_x g(\mathbf J_x-\mathbf R_x)$ to factor through
$\partial_x g\,\mathbf G$ on the left and $\mathbf G^\top$
on the right. The image condition states that every
transverse internal motion can be corrected by the
available self-feedback directions. The kernel condition
states that an energy-gradient direction invisible at the
feedback output cannot generate a transverse motion.
The equivalence holds pointwise. Constant ranks of
$\partial_x g\,\mathbf G$ and $\mathbf G$ locally permit
a smooth choice of $\mathbf Q$.

Passivity imposes a further restriction on the matrices.
If $\mathbf Q+\mathbf Q^\top\preceq0$, the symmetric part
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:structural}, multiplied on the right by
$(\partial_x g)^\top$, yields
\begin{equation}
\mathbf R_x(\partial_x g)^\top=0.
\label{eq:dissipation}
\end{equation}
To see this, the closed-loop dissipation matrix
$\mathbf R_x-\mathbf G(\mathbf Q+\mathbf Q^\top)
\mathbf G^\top/2$ is positive semidefinite, whereas
the remaining closed-loop matrix is skew-symmetric.
Their projections along the constraint normals must
therefore vanish separately. The reversible part must
also satisfy
\begin{equation}
\left[
\mathbf J_x+
\tfrac12\mathbf G(\mathbf Q-\mathbf Q^\top)\mathbf G^\top
\right](\partial_x g)^\top=0.
\label{eq:reversible}
\end{equation}
Thus passive structural invariance requires the constraint
normals to be null directions of both the dissipative and
effective reversible generators. When the effective
skew matrix satisfies the Jacobi identity, the
components of $g$ are its Casimir functions.
The dissipative restriction is related to the
dissipation obstacle in port-Hamiltonian
control by interconnection \cite{zhang2015}.


The Hamiltonian structure makes viability a physical
restriction, rather than only a geometric one. For example,
suppose continued operation requires $H_x(x)\ge E_*$.
At a viable state where $H_x=E_*$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:viability}
requires $\dot H_x\ge0$. Equation~\eqref{eq:x} then yields
\begin{equation}
y^\top u+y_x^\top u_x
\ge
(\nabla_x H)^\top\mathbf R_x\nabla_x H.
\label{eq:energetic}
\end{equation}
The environmental exchange and self-feedback must jointly
compensate the system's dissipation at this boundary.
This inequality is necessary, not sufficient: the
same motion must also respect every other active constraint.
Other physically defined viable sets give other conditions.

A closed dynamics is \emph{self-maintaining} when it
preserves a physically defined set in which its regulating
mechanisms remain available. Geometric viability,
feedback compatibility, and the power balance constrain
that behavior at different levels. Their relation to
organizational closure \cite{montevil2015} depends on
the physical processes that maintain those mechanisms.
